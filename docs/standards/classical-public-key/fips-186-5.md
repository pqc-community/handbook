## What it covers

Federal Information Processing Standards Publication 186-5 (FIPS 186-5) specifies NIST's classical digital signature algorithms: the Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (ECDSA), the Edwards-Curve Digital Signature Algorithm (EdDSA, specified in RFC 8032) and RSA signatures (specified in RFC 8017, PKCS #1 v2.2). The elliptic curves used with ECDSA and EdDSA are specified separately in [NIST SP 800-186](/standards/classical-public-key/sp-800-186/). FIPS 186-5 covers signatures only; it does not specify key establishment.

FIPS 186-5 was issued by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) and superseded FIPS 186-4. NIST's FIPS compliance FAQ (content last updated July 3, 2024) says FIPS are standards for federal computer systems and are not always mandatory for federal agencies: each FIPS's applicability section says when it is mandatory. FIPS do not apply to national security systems, and private-sector organizations use them voluntarily, although contractors with government contracts must comply with FISMA, which mandates the use of FIPS.

## Relationship with other standards

[NIST IR 8547](/standards/transition-and-key-management/nist-ir-8547/), final October 2026, identifies the FIPS 186-5 algorithms as vulnerable to Shor's algorithm on a cryptanalytically relevant quantum computer. It lists [FIPS 204](/standards/post-quantum/fips-204/) (ML-DSA) and [FIPS 205](/standards/post-quantum/fips-205/) (SLH-DSA) as quantum-resistant digital signature standards, alongside the stateful hash-based schemes in [SP 800-208](/standards/post-quantum/sp-800-208/). The 2030 and 2035 dates are NIST's schedule for its own standards and guidelines; for US federal high value assets and high-impact systems, [EO 14412](/standards/us-government-requirements/eo-14412/) sets December 31, 2031, for post-quantum signatures, and [OMB M-26-15](/standards/us-government-requirements/omb-m-26-15/) phases agencies' work to meet it. Other authorities set their own dates; see [Why migration timelines differ](/learn/explainers/why-timelines-differ/).

IR 8547 notes that signatures are not vulnerable to harvest-now, decrypt-later attacks, but treats their transition as urgent because it takes longer and needs more parties to coordinate. Long-lived verifiers, such as root certificates and keys built into devices to check firmware, need separate planning from everyday signing. During the transition, IR 8547 says NIST standards and guidelines accommodate dual signatures provided at least one component is NIST-approved (§3.2.2). See [PKI and certificates](/technology-options/pki/) and [Firmware and secure boot](/technology-options/firmware-secure-boot/).
