## What it covers

RFC 10073 defines composite ML-DSA: a post-quantum/traditional (PQ/T) hybrid signature scheme that combines ML-DSA with a traditional algorithm — RSASSA-PKCS1-v1_5, RSASSA-PSS, ECDSA, Ed25519 or Ed448 — under a single algorithm identifier for X.509 and PKIX structures. A composite signature is accepted only if all its component signatures verify, so an operator gains protection against a break or implementation flaw in ML-DSA while keeping a classical algorithm in place. The RFC says the combinations are tailored to meet regulatory guidelines in certain regions.

RFC 10073 was published by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) in November 2026 as a Proposed Standard on the Standards Track. IETF RFCs are voluntary technical specifications; RFC 10073 does not oblige anyone to use hybrid signatures. The RFC's own note on FIPS approval says it is not authoritative and has not been endorsed by NIST.

## Relationship with other standards

RFC 10073 builds on [RFC 9881](/standards/modules-protocols-certificates/rfc-9881/) and [FIPS 204](/standards/post-quantum/fips-204/). Its companion, RFC 10082 (Proposed Standard, November 2026), defines the same composite signatures for the Cryptographic Message Syntax (CMS), which carries signed code, documents and email. [NIST IR 8547](/standards/transition-and-key-management/nist-ir-8547/) (§3.2.2) says dual or composite signature schemes are accommodated if at least one component is approved, and (§3.2) that hybrids are typically temporary and lead to a second transition later.

Authorities differ on whether hybrid signatures are needed. Germany's BSI TR-02102-1 recommends post-quantum signatures only together with classical ones (with exceptions for hash-based schemes); France's ANSSI strongly stresses hybridation where post-quantum protection is needed; the US NSA's CNSA 2.0 does not require hybrid products for national security systems. See [National algorithm positions](/standards/national-algorithm-positions/) and [Hybrid approaches](/learn/hybrid/). Composite signatures mainly matter where signatures must be trusted for a long time or verified by devices that are hard to update; they do not affect key establishment.
