On October 14, 2026, Singapore's Cyber Security Agency (CSA) published an [Advisory on Quantum-Safe Migration for Organisations](https://www.csa.gov.sg/alerts-and-advisories/advisories/advisory-on-quantum-safe-migration-for-organisations/). It applies broadly to organizations that rely on digital systems, data or communications, rather than only to critical infrastructure, and sets out six steps drawn from CSA's own migration handbook, alongside dated targets that it recommends rather than requires.

{/* truncate */}

## What CSA published

The advisory, summarized on the handbook's [CSA advisory page](/standards/migration-guides/csa-quantum-safe-advisory/), is written for organizations generally: CSA says it "applies broadly to organisations reliant on digital systems, data, or communications for their operations". Owners of Critical Information Infrastructure (CII) are directed instead to the guidance CSA issued to them in June 2026, which the advisory says sets a 2031 target for migrating CII systems.

For everyone else, CSA says organizations "should aim to have a migration plan in place by end 2027", and that other organizations exposed to quantum risks "are encouraged to complete migration by 2035". These are recommendations from a national authority to its own audience, not legal obligations, and they sit beside, rather than replace, the dates other authorities set for theirs; the handbook's [migration guides](/standards/migration-guides/) collect those other positions.

## The six steps

The steps are drawn from CSA's Quantum-Safe Migration Handbook, and CSA also points organizations to its Quantum Readiness Index, a self-assessment tool for finding capability gaps. In order, the advisory asks organizations to understand their risk and identify their most important systems, starting with data that must stay confidential for years, internet-facing systems and long-lived equipment that is hard to update; to establish clear ownership, with a named person accountable for the migration; and to identify, evaluate and test replacement algorithms for priority systems before full rollout, because larger keys affect performance.

It then asks them to engage vendors and partners about product support, upgrade roadmaps, timelines, responsibility and cost, and to favor vendors that are preparing when contracts are renewed; to build awareness among leadership and internal and outsourced IT teams; and to build in cryptographic agility, so that algorithms can be swapped with minimal disruption and are not hard-coded.

## What it means for your plan

The advisory is one of the few authority publications aimed at organizations generally rather than at large or critical estates, and its steps line up closely with the handbook's [core actions](/plan/core-actions/): name an owner, list what you use on the register, and put post-quantum wording into every purchase and renewal. Its call for cryptographic agility goes further than the core actions and is closer to the program's [keep the ability to change again](/plan/program/changing-algorithms-again/) step. If your organization operates in Singapore, record CSA's end-2027 and 2035 targets in your register as CSA's, with the audience they apply to, and check [which dates apply to you](/plan/#which-dates-apply-to-you) before adopting them anywhere else.

Its first step is a useful prompt for organizations that are unsure whether they need more than the core actions. Data that must stay confidential for years and equipment that cannot easily be updated are close to the cases the [four questions](/plan/) send to a program, and the advisory's supplier questions match those in [Work with suppliers](/plan/program/suppliers/).
