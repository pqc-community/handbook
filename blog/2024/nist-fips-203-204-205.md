On 13 August 2024 the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) [published its first three post-quantum cryptography standards](https://csrc.nist.gov/News/2024/postquantum-cryptography-fips-approved) as final Federal Information Processing Standards: FIPS 203 for key establishment, and FIPS 204 and FIPS 205 for digital signatures. They give products, protocols and purchasers a stable specification to build on, but they standardise algorithms rather than set migration dates.

## What NIST published

The Secretary of Commerce approved the three standards, which NIST had released as drafts for public comment on 24 August 2023. Each one specifies an algorithm derived from a submission to NIST's post-quantum standardisation process:

- [FIPS 203](/standards/post-quantum/fips-203/), the Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard, specifies ML-KEM, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber, which two parties use to establish a shared secret key;
- [FIPS 204](/standards/post-quantum/fips-204/), the Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Standard, specifies ML-DSA, derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium;
- [FIPS 205](/standards/post-quantum/fips-205/), the Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Standard, specifies SLH-DSA, derived from SPHINCS+, whose security rests on hash functions rather than lattices.

NIST describes ML-DSA as its primary standard for digital signatures and SLH-DSA as a backup method in case ML-DSA proves vulnerable. It also says a fourth standard, based on FALCON, is in development, and it encourages system administrators to begin transitioning to the new standards as soon as possible. The [algorithms lesson](/learn/algorithms/) explains which job each algorithm does and why their keys and signatures are larger than the ones they replace.

## A standard is not a deadline

A FIPS standardises an algorithm; it does not tell organisations when to stop using the old ones. NIST's compliance FAQ describes FIPS as standards for federal computer systems, says they do not apply to national security systems, and notes that private-sector organisations use them voluntarily. Dates for moving away from RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography belong to named authorities and their audiences, so these standards alone do not create a migration deadline for anyone outside their scope, as [Why migration timelines differ](/learn/explainers/why-timelines-differ/) explains.

The two jobs also keep their own clocks. ML-KEM addresses key establishment, where the urgency comes from data that must stay confidential long enough to be worth recording and decrypting later. ML-DSA and SLH-DSA address signatures, where the timetable depends on when every verifier in a chain can check the new algorithms.

## What it means for your plan

Final standards change what you can ask for. When you talk to suppliers, ask whether their post-quantum support implements the final FIPS versions rather than the earlier Kyber or Dilithium drafts, and from which release; the [purchase wording record](/templates/records/purchase-wording/) gives wording for purchases and renewals. Record the answers as they stand, with `unknown` where a supplier has not said.

Products will need time to implement and validate the standards, and protocols such as TLS still need their own specifications before the algorithms can be used on the network. Keep key establishment and signatures as separate rows in your [register](/templates/records/register/), because a supplier can deliver one well before the other.
